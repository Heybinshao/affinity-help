# Recovering and preserving highlights - Affinity Help Center

> 官方来源：https://www.affinity.studio/help/recovering-preserving-highlights/

Understanding how to preserve and recover highlights helps maintain tonal balance, improve realism, and retain image quality throughout your editing workflow.

![Image 1: Example of clipped highlights preview (red overlay) in the Develop Studio.](https://images.ctfassets.net/3p2fxa94bzao/4hGn6uOw4y51KfPTdaf7lL/d4dbc601cb7b48ae04ce13627a398df7/clippedHighlights.jpg)

Example of clipped highlights preview (red overlay) in the Develop Studio.

Blown highlights are most commonly caused by overexposure — when scene brightness exceeds what the camera sensor can record at the chosen exposure setting. Sensors can only capture a finite range of tonal values (dynamic range), and when the brightest areas push beyond that limit, those pixels default to pure white with no recoverable data. High-contrast scenes, such as a bright sky against a shaded foreground, are especially prone to this.

When highlights become clipped, they appear as pure white areas with no visible texture or detail. Once clipped, highlight detail cannot always be fully restored. This is particularly important when printing your photos, as the areas with clipped highlights (pure white) would not be printed thus reducing the final image quality.

You can work on restoring detail in highlights in **Develop Studio**, which is the default environment for editing RAW files, and further refine them in **Pixel Studio** with the use of adjustment and filter layers.

Preserving highlights ensures that:

*   Bright areas retain texture and depth.
*   Images maintain natural tonal transitions.
*   Skin tones remain realistic.
*   Sky and light sources do not appear flat or blown out.
*   Dynamic range remains balanced.

Overexposed highlights can reduce the overall impact of an image and limit flexibility during editing. By protecting highlight detail, you preserve more tonal information for later refinement.

Clipped highlights appear as areas of solid white without variation. To identify clipping:

*   Review bright areas visually.
*   Use the **Histogram**panel to check for tonal data pressed against the right edge.
*   Watch for sudden loss of detail in skies, clouds, or reflective surfaces.

When editing, highlight recovery is typically performed during global tonal adjustments. Consider the following when editing globally:

*   **Highlights**(Shadows/Highlights adjustment) to restore detail in bright areas.
*   **Exposure**to reduce overall brightness.
*   **Curves**to gently lower the highlight range.
*   **Levels**to adjust the white point carefully.

For localized clipping, apply:

*   A masked adjustment layer.
*   **Blend Options**to target only highlight regions.
*   Subtle Dodge & Burn refinements to rebalance tonal contrast.
